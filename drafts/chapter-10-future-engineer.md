# Chapter 10: The Future Software Engineer

From programmer to knowledge engineer

> The defining skill of tomorrow's software engineer will not be the ability to write code. It will be the ability to understand complex systems well enough that code becomes almost inevitable.

## The question everyone asks

Whenever a major technological shift occurs, the same question follows.

When compilers became popular, people asked: "Will programmers still be necessary?"

When fourth-generation languages appeared: "Will analysts replace developers?"

When low-code platforms emerged: "Will business users build everything?"

Now AI has revived the question.

"Will software engineers become obsolete?"

History suggests the answer is no: roles rarely disappear. They evolve.

## Every abstraction changes the profession

Consider a programmer in 1965.

A significant part of their expertise involved understanding:

- Processor registers
- Memory layouts
- Assembly instructions
- Hardware limitations

Today, most developers never think about these topics.

The abstraction layers above them have become so effective that hardware programming is a specialised discipline rather than a universal requirement.

Did software engineering disappear? No. Its focus changed, and the same pattern has repeated throughout history.

## From syntax to semantics

For decades, software engineering education emphasised syntax.

Students learned:

- Java
- C++
- Python
- SQL
- JavaScript

Frameworks became equally important.

- Spring
- Angular
- React
- Hibernate
- Entity Framework

The implicit assumption was simple.

If you can write code, you can build software.

AI challenges that assumption.

Syntax is becoming inexpensive.

Meaning remains difficult.

## The new scarcity

Economics teaches that value follows scarcity.

When something becomes abundant, its value declines.

Consider handwritten calculations.

Before calculators, arithmetic was valuable. After calculators, arithmetic remained useful but ceased to be a defining professional skill.

Instead, understanding mathematics became more valuable.

Programming is undergoing a similar transition.

Generating syntax becomes abundant.

Understanding business systems remains scarce.

That scarcity increases its value.

## The engineer as interpreter

Future software engineers will spend much of their time translating between worlds.

Business experts speak in terms of goals. Architects speak in terms of systems. Developers speak in terms of implementation. AI speaks in terms of context.

Someone must ensure that all four describe the same reality.

That translator is the software engineer, an interpreter rather than merely a programmer.

## Requirements engineering returns

For many years, requirements engineering lost prestige.

Many organisations viewed it as bureaucracy. Developers wanted to build. Business stakeholders wanted demonstrations. Documentation often became an afterthought.

AI changes the equation.

Poor requirements now produce poor software at unprecedented speed. Conversely, excellent requirements produce excellent software at unprecedented speed.

Requirements engineering is no longer a preliminary activity.

It becomes one of the highest-leverage disciplines in software development.

## Domain knowledge becomes a competitive advantage

Suppose two developers have equal experience with Java.

One has spent ten years working in banking. The other has never worked in finance.

Historically, both could eventually implement the same feature. The difference lay mainly in development speed.

In an AI-native environment, something changes.

The AI can generate much of the implementation.

What it cannot generate is deep understanding of one organisation's business.

The banking expert becomes dramatically more valuable, not because they write better loops but because they ask better questions.

## Architecture becomes more important

Some fear that AI diminishes architecture.

The opposite is more likely.

Poor architecture allows every generated feature to drift.

Strong architecture provides stable constraints within which AI can operate safely.

Architects increasingly become designers of organisational knowledge rather than reviewers of code.

Their decisions influence thousands of future AI-generated implementations.

This increases the leverage of architectural thinking.

## Code review evolves

Today's code reviews often focus on formatting, naming, style, boilerplate, and minor refactoring.

Much of this can already be automated.

Future reviews will focus more heavily on questions such as:

- Does this implementation reflect the business rule?
- Does the specification contain ambiguity?
- Has the AI misunderstood an important concept?
- Are the architectural constraints still appropriate?

The review shifts from syntax towards intent.

## The new skills

If we were designing a software engineering curriculum for 2035, it might look rather different.

Traditional topics would remain:

- Algorithms
- Data structures
- Networking
- Operating systems
- Databases

But new disciplines would become equally important.

**Requirements Engineering:** how do we discover and describe business needs?

**Domain Modelling:** how do we represent complex organisations accurately?

**Context Engineering:** how do we organise knowledge so AI can use it effectively?

**AI Governance:** how do we validate AI-generated implementations?

**Systems Thinking:** how do individual decisions affect entire organisations?

These are engineering skills.

They simply operate at a higher level of abstraction.

## Communication is technical

Software engineers have often underestimated communication.

Many regarded it as a "soft skill."

AI exposes this misconception.

Describing a business precisely enough that another intelligence can implement it is an extraordinarily technical activity.

Precision, consistency, completeness, and structure are engineering qualities, not merely communication skills.

## The rise of the knowledge engineer

A useful way to think about the future profession is through a new title.

Knowledge Engineer.

A knowledge engineer:

- Discovers business knowledge
- Organises it
- Validates it
- Models it
- Maintains it
- Enables machines to act upon it

Code remains important.

But code becomes one expression of structured knowledge rather than the knowledge itself.

## What AI cannot easily replace

Whenever technology advances, it is useful to ask: What remains fundamentally human?

Several capabilities stand out.

**Judgement:** choosing between competing priorities.

**Negotiation:** resolving conflicting stakeholder needs.

**Ethics:** evaluating consequences beyond technical correctness.

**Leadership:** helping teams navigate uncertainty.

**Creativity:** reframing problems rather than merely solving them.

**Domain Expertise:** understanding industries, organisations and people.

AI assists with all of these without eliminating the need for them.

## The risk of over-reliance

This book has emphasised the opportunities created by AI.

Balance requires acknowledging the risks.

Organisations that depend entirely on generated code without maintaining strong specifications may create a new form of technical debt.

Instead of undocumented code, they produce undocumented prompts.

Future engineers inherit conversations rather than knowledge.

This is no improvement.

The discipline described throughout this book exists precisely to avoid that outcome.

## Engineering judgement remains central

Suppose an AI proposes three possible implementations.

All satisfy the specification.

Which should be chosen?

Perhaps one performs better. Perhaps another is easier to maintain. Perhaps a third better supports future business change.

Specifications narrow the design space.

They rarely eliminate judgement entirely.

Engineering remains a profession because judgement cannot be fully automated.

## A more humane profession

There is another possibility that receives less attention.

Many software engineers entered the profession because they enjoyed solving interesting problems.

Yet much of their time is spent on repetitive work:

- Wiring dependencies
- Writing CRUD operations
- Configuring frameworks
- Translating specifications into boilerplate

If AI absorbs much of this routine effort, engineers gain more time for the activities that originally attracted many of them to the field:

- Understanding businesses
- Designing systems
- Improving architectures
- Collaborating with users
- Solving difficult problems

The profession may become not only more productive, but also more intellectually rewarding.

## Advice for new engineers

If you are beginning your career, what should you learn?

Learn programming. You still need to understand how software works.

But do not stop there.

Study:

- Business analysis
- Software architecture
- Distributed systems
- Security
- Domain-driven design
- Requirements engineering
- Communication
- AI-assisted development

Programming will remain necessary.

It simply will not be sufficient.

## Advice for experienced engineers

If you have spent years writing software, resist the temptation to compete with AI at what AI increasingly does well.

Instead, cultivate the capabilities that become more valuable over time.

- Deep domain expertise
- Architectural judgement
- Clear communication
- Mentoring
- Systems thinking

These are difficult to automate because they depend upon experience rather than syntax.

## The next generation of software engineering

Every technological revolution changes what it means to be an expert.

The expert carpenter is not the one who can sharpen an axe fastest. The expert architect is not the one who mixes concrete most efficiently.

Likewise, the future software engineer will not be measured primarily by typing speed or memorised APIs.

They will be measured by their ability to transform organisational knowledge into reliable, adaptable software systems.

AI changes the tools, not the purpose.

## Looking ahead

We have now explored the principles, practices and professional implications of AI-native, specification-driven development.

One chapter remains.

It asks a broader question.

What happens if this methodology becomes the norm?

How will software itself change?

How will organisations change?

And what might software engineering look like twenty years from now?

The final chapter looks beyond today's tools and considers the long-term implications of building software from knowledge rather than code.
